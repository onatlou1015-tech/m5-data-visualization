📊 What is data visualization? core idea

Data visualization is the process of transforming data into diagrams, charts, or graphs that make information easy to understand, accurate, clear, and engaging.

🌟 Why visualise data?
• Understand complex patterns quickly
• Communicate insights to others clearly
• Persuade with evidence and clarity
• Discover trends and outliers you might miss in raw numbers

📈 Chart types choose wisely

🥧 Pie Chart Show parts of a whole (percentages). Best for 3–5 categories.
📊 Bar Chart Compare categories. Easy to read — great for ranking.
📈 Line Graph Show trends over time. Continuous data.
🔵 Scatter Plot Show relationship between two variables. Look for correlation.
📌 Quick guide:
  • Compare categories → Bar chart
  • Show parts of a whole → Pie chart (only if few categories!)
  • Show change over time → Line graph
  • Show relationship between variables → Scatter plot

9.1 Hook – “What story does this map tell?” 10 min

🗺️ New York City Running Map

💡 Think like: 🚦 “If you were a traffic officer, how would you use this map?” · 🍜 “If you owned a Thai restaurant, how would you use this map to plan?”

🚦 Traffic Officer's Perspective:

A traffic police officer in New York City could use this running map as a powerful tool for improving road safety and managing traffic flow. The darker lines on the map indicate areas with high runner traffic, which also means these are locations with heavy pedestrian activity. The officer would focus on intersections along these busy running routes, identifying where runners and vehicles frequently cross paths. By understanding these patterns, the officer could deploy additional personnel during peak running hours—typically early mornings and evenings—to help direct traffic and ensure pedestrian safety. The map also helps in planning for road closures during major running events like marathons, allowing the officer to anticipate congestion and communicate detours to drivers in advance. Furthermore, by analyzing multiple maps over time, the officer could spot seasonal trends, such as increased running activity in spring or summer, and adjust traffic management strategies accordingly. In emergency situations, the map provides valuable insight into which areas are most crowded with runners, enabling faster emergency response and safer route planning. Ultimately, this simple visualization transforms raw data into actionable intelligence, helping the officer make smarter, data-driven decisions that protect both runners and motorists.

🍜 Restaurant Owner's Perspective:

A Thai restaurant owner in New York City could use this running map as a strategic business tool to attract more customers and increase sales. The darker lines on the map indicate areas with high runner traffic, which represent concentrations of active, health-conscious individuals who are likely to be hungry after their workouts. The restaurant owner would identify these busy running routes—particularly in and around Central Park—and consider placing a food truck, pop-up stall, or promotional signage near these high-traffic areas during peak running hours, such as early mornings or weekends. The map also helps the owner understand the flow of foot traffic, allowing them to choose a permanent restaurant location that is easily accessible to runners, perhaps near popular starting or ending points of running routes. Additionally, by observing patterns over time, the owner could adjust their menu or marketing strategy, offering post-run specials like protein-rich Thai dishes or refreshing drinks that appeal to athletes. The map even provides insights for delivery planning—if runners frequently pass by a certain area, the owner could target that neighborhood with flyers or delivery promotions. Ultimately, the running map transforms from a simple visual into a valuable guide for customer acquisition, location planning, and targeted marketing, helping the restaurant owner make informed decisions that align with the habits and needs of their potential customers.

9.3 Tables vs. Charts – “Which one should you use?” 15 min

When should you use a table, and when should you use a chart? The answer depends on what you want your audience to see and do with the data.

📋 Use a TABLE when...

  • You need to show exact values that people need to look up or compare precisely
  • You have many categories (more than 10) that would crowd a chart
  • Your audience needs to read individual numbers or perform calculations
  • You are presenting detailed financial data, scores, or measurements
  • You want to show multiple attributes for each item side-by-side

📊 Use a CHART when...

  • You want to show patterns, trends, or relationships in the data
  • You need to compare categories quickly and visually
  • Your audience needs to understand the big picture at a glance
  • You want to persuade or tell a story with the data
  • You are presenting to a general audience who may not need exact numbers

💡 Real-world scenario

Imagine you are the principal of a school. You have data on 500 students showing:

  • Each student's name, grade level, and exam scores for 5 subjects
  • The average score for each subject across the whole school

Question: Which would you use to tell the story, and which for detailed records?

✅ Answer:
  • Chart (bar chart or line graph) → To quickly show which subjects have the highest/lowest average scores and identify patterns.
  • Table → To keep a detailed record of each student's individual scores for report cards and parent meetings.
💡 Key insight: Tables are for exact values — charts are for patterns and stories.

9.6 Appropriate Visualization – “What makes a visualisation effective?” 10 min

A visualisation is "appropriate" when it successfully communicates the right message to the right audience in the clearest way possible. Here are the key principles:

🎯 Clear Purpose Know what story you're telling before you start.
👥 Know Your Audience Experts need detail; general audiences need simplicity.
📊 Right Chart Type Match the chart to the data and the message.
🎨 Clean & Simple Remove clutter. Highlight what matters.
🌈 Colour with Purpose Use colour to guide attention, not to decorate.
📝 Label Clearly Titles, axes, and legends should be easy to read and understand.

⚠️ Common mistakes to avoid

  • Too much information – cramming too many categories or data points into one chart
  • Misleading scales – starting a bar chart at a value other than zero (it distorts differences!)
  • 3D charts – they look fancy but often make data harder to read accurately
  • Too many colours – using rainbow colours when a simple palette would be clearer
  • Missing labels – not labelling axes, units, or data points
💡 Key insight: The best visualisation is not the most complex — it's the one that makes the data understandable to your audience.
